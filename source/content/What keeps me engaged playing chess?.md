## My notes

### **Nov 10, 2026 (voice note)**

So now we know that the way to get better at chess is building these representations while being faced with different positions. Playing blitz doesn't really support that. You need to give yourself room to pay attention to the position and to make your own judgments. What do you notice about it? How would you respond? How would your opponent respond? During a blitz game, you simply don't have time to form new mental images. You're basically playing on autopilot, making decisions based on the representations you've already built. And if there aren't many, then you're simply playing on instinct. There's not enough time for you to notice what's important about the position. So playing blitz only tests what you already know and what you've already paid a lot of attention to. That means that in order to give yourself the space to build new representations, you would have to play longer games, like rapid games. Or, if you don't play games, go through a book of annotated games and cover the annotations. Cover the moves that were played, and only read until it's your turn and you have to make a move.

- I think when I encounter a particular playing style, point of view or an idea that intrigues me. When I don't like a chess book, I pick another one. You don't have to read Mastering Chess Strategy just because it is highly recommended. Again, diversity is key, changing the relationship to the game. I found that analyzing games with an engine doesn't keep me engaged, I don't like it, I feel a certain resistance.
- So, I discovered this author Emmanuel Neiman. The description of his book The Magnus Method caught my attention.
- What does Magnus Carlsen bring to the game?
- Do chess engines evaluate positions with respect to how likely it is that you can go wrong?

From the description of The Magnus Method:

> He explains a key element in the World Champion’s play: instead of the ‘absolute’ best move **he often plays the move that is likely to give him the better chances.** Carlsen’s singular ability to win positions that are equal or only very slightly favourable comes down to this: he doesn’t let his opponents get what they hope for while offering them the maximum amount of chances to go wrong.

> In areas such as pawn play, piece play, exchanges as a positional weapon and breaking the rules in endgames, Neiman shows that **Magnus Carlsen has brought a new understanding to the game.** Neiman also looks at Carlsen’s key qualities that are not directly related to technique. Such as his **unparalleled fighting spirit** and **his ability to objectively evaluate any kind of position and situation**. Carlsen is **extremely widely read and knows basically everything about chess.** What’s more, as the **most versatile player** in the history of the game he is **totally unpredictable**.

My follow-up note on engines (answer from Perplexity): If you want “which move gives my opponent the greatest chance to go wrong?”, you need a practical-choice framework: prefer forcing moves, limited opponent choices, familiar structures, and lines you can calculate confidently—not merely the engine’s top evaluation.

**Aug 8, 2026 (voice note)**

When you want to get better at playing chess, first of all, you play chess. I think you can learn a lot by watching other players, copying some of their ideas and trying to apply them to your own game. It’s important that you try to guess their intention behind an idea and even try it in contexts where it might not work. Yesterday I watched Magnus Carlsen play a game against his 20-year-old self. I think they played the Rossolimo Sicilian or something, and there was an idea he played that I tried to apply in my French game today. I was Black. His pawn was on d3, and I played d4 because I wanted to prevent him from playing d4 to close the center, which might make the game very drawish. So I copied this idea, even though it’s a bad move from an engine’s perspective. But it’s a human game. I’m not trying to make the most accurate move; I’m trying to play a game. I want to try ideas even if they might not work, because learning how an idea fails teaches me a lot about how my opponent would respond and defend. Even making mistakes teaches me a lot about the mechanics of the game: how an idea fails, and what happens when you try things in a position.
